PLI is a sealed-envelope service for mutual interest. Everyone in a group privately names the people they hope feel the same. If two people name each other, each receives the other's email address. If not, nothing happens — and no one, including us in any practical sense we can avoid, ever finds out who named whom.

The rules, always

  1. You may name up to three people by email address. The three are final — no edits, no withdrawals, no viewing your list.
  2. The people you name are never told. Not now, not later, not ever.
  3. At the published reveal time, mutual pairs — and only mutual pairs — each receive the other's address. One message. That is all.
  4. Everything else is deleted, unread and unmatched.
  5. If a round doesn't reach its published threshold, it is voided and everything is deleted. Nothing is revealed to anyone.

Take part

PLI runs as rounds inside communities and events: a campus week, a conference, a speed-dating night. You join through the link the organizer circulates. Some events are listed publicly:

Run one

Organizers create an event, set its own timeline — opens, closes, reveal — restrict it to email domains or a join code, and circulate the link. Organizers see a signup count and the round status. They never see who joined, who named whom, or how many matched: that information is not retained in a form anyone can show them.

Organizer sign-in

The trust you are extending

Addresses are stored as keyed hashes and encrypted blobs, so a copy of the database alone reveals nothing. But the operator holds the key material and could, technically, reconstruct who named whom during a live round. The graph is destroyed at reveal. The source code is public. That is the trust you are extending. If that is not enough — and that is a legitimate position — do not play.

No profiles. No photos. No chat. No browsing. No scores. If you both said it, you both find out. Otherwise, nothing happened.
